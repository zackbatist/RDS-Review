Research Protocol

NoteStatus

Draft. The eligibility criteria and limits can still change. The protocol will be registered on OSF Registries before screening begins, because PROSPERO does not accept scoping reviews. Registration link: to be added.

Background

This review maps the factors reported to influence the sharing of research data in health research, including barriers, facilitators, and governance mechanisms.

This review updates the 2019 review of research data sharing (Chawinga and Zinn 2019) with the scoping review methods of Cavallaro et al. (2026). The 2019 review shares this review’s focus on research data sharing, which makes it a strong candidate for an update. It looks at differences across countries. This review looks at differences across research domains, with special attention on healthcare.

Earlier reviews address parts of this topic. Reviews in health and medical research examine incentives (Rowhani-Farid et al. 2017), principles and norms (Kalkman et al. 2019), and governance mechanisms such as harmonized data access agreements (Saulnier et al. 2019). Reviews in public health examine barriers to public health data sharing but exclude research data sharing (van Panhuis et al. 2014).

This review compares health research data sharing with data sharing in general and in other fields. Beyond health, cross-disciplinary reviews cover what drives and inhibits researchers in sharing and reusing data (Fecher et al. 2015; Zuiderwijk et al. 2020; Perrier et al. 2020).

The taxonomy of factors this review produces serves the CITF-emails study, where it supports manual annotation for supervised machine learning on the emails and identifies factors that the emails cover only in part.

Objective and questions

The objective is to map the factors reported to influence the sharing of research data in health research, and to compare them with the factors reported for other fields and for public health data.

  1. Which factors (barriers, facilitators, and governance mechanisms) are reported to influence the sharing of research data in health research?
  2. How can these factors be organized into a taxonomy suitable for annotating correspondence about data sharing?
  3. How do the factors reported for health research differ from those reported for research in other fields and for public health data?

Methods

The review follows the scoping review framework of Arksey and O’Malley (2005) as refined by Levac et al. (2010) and the JBI guidance (Peters et al. 2020). The method draws on the protocol of Cavallaro et al. (2026), which pairs machine learning screening with automated deduplication. It improves on that protocol in deduplication and seed generation. The review is reported according to PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al. 2018). The searches are reported according to PRISMA-S (Rethlefsen et al. 2021). The flow diagram adapts the PRISMA 2020 template (Page et al. 2021), with “sources of evidence” in place of “studies”.

Eligibility criteria

The criteria follow the population, concept, and context (PCC) structure that JBI recommends for scoping reviews.

Element Criterion
Population No restriction. Sources may concern researchers, data stewards, institutions, or governance bodies.
Concept Factors that influence the sharing of research data, including barriers, facilitators, and governance mechanisms. Research data are data used for research, whether collected for a study or collected for another purpose and reused for research. Data governance comprises the strategies and tools employed to identify, manage, and disseminate data (Leonelli 2019). In health research, data access governance manages who has access to data, for what purposes, and under what conditions, through data access committees, data access agreements, policies, and monitoring (Shabani et al. 2021).
Context, tier 1 Health research. All source types are eligible.
Context, tier 2 Research in other fields and cross-disciplinary research. All source types are eligible.

Sources are excluded when they concern public health data, health systems data, or clinical care data used only for surveillance, service delivery, or patient care. Sources that concern these data reused for research are eligible. The data_origin variable in the codebook records whether a source concerns data collected for research or data reused for research after collection for surveillance, service delivery, or patient care. Each source is assigned a tier at screening from its field.

Sources that design, build or evaluate a specific system, product or service for sharing data are excluded, such as a blockchain framework, a cloud platform or an encryption scheme. Sources that study why people adopt or resist such a system report factors and are eligible.

The search covers sources published from 2015 to 2025 in English and French, the languages the reviewers read.

Information sources and search

One query runs in Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed. It searches titles for a form of share within five words of data, which matches “data sharing” along with phrasings such as “sharing research data” and “sharing of health data”, in sources published from 2015 to 2025 in English and French. It applies no document type filter, since all source types are eligible. The search strategy page gives the query as written for each database, with the number of records it returns.

Candidate queries were compared on the records they return and on how many known relevant sources they find. The known sources are the sources included in the review by Chawinga and Zinn (2019) that meet this review’s criteria, apart from publication year. Those published from 2007 to 2014 served to compare candidate queries, and those published from 2015 were set aside until the query was chosen, to test it on sources it was not built from. A title search returned a small fraction of the records that a search of titles and abstracts returned and found most of the known sources. Factor terms, such as barriers, incentives, and policies, found few more known sources for many more records. The screening guide lists them for reviewers to apply when they screen. The comparisons also showed that about a third of the records in Scopus and Web of Science report a system, product, or service for sharing data, such as a blockchain framework or a cloud storage scheme, which the eligibility criteria exclude. The query leaves out titles that name blockchain, smart contracts, the Internet of Things, vehicular networks, edge computing, or cloud computing, the words that most often mark such sources. Known sources the query misses are added to the records by hand. The search strategy page reports how the query was developed and tested, and lists the known sources added by hand.

Selection of sources

Records are deduplicated with bib-dedupe (Wagner 2024), which, unlike the Zotero workflow of Cavallaro et al. (2026), runs from a script and makes the step reproducible. The records are then screened on title and abstract in ASReview, and again on full text, against the screening guide. Exclusion reasons are recorded at full text. Counts at every stage feed the flow diagram.

ASReview ranks records by predicted relevance with its default model, ELAS u5, which pairs TF-IDF features with a support vector machine. The ranking starts from a seed sample of 200 records drawn at random, with a fixed random seed, from the deduplicated records. The sample size follows the evaluation of the stopping criterion below (Callaghan and Müller-Hansen 2020). The share of relevant records in the seed sample estimates the share across all records, which indicates how much screening the stopping criterion will require. Cavallaro et al. (2026) select their seed sample ad hoc from the first 5% of deduplicated records.

Two reviewers independently screen the titles and abstracts of all records in the seed sample and resolve disagreements through discussion. The annotated seed sample initializes the ASReview ranking. One reviewer screens the ranked records until the stopping rule applies. Both reviewers then independently screen the full texts of the included records and resolve disagreements through discussion.

Screening in ASReview stops on the statistical criterion of Callaghan and Müller-Hansen (2020), in its ranked form. After each screening session, the decisions are exported in the order they were made, seed sample first, and a hypergeometric test on them checks whether recall has reached a target of 95% at a confidence level of 95%. Screening stops when the test rejects the hypothesis that recall falls below the target. In an evaluation of 15 stopping methods on 81 datasets, this criterion alone never stopped before its recall target (Repke et al. 2026), while fixed stopping rules in ASReview fail to generalize across datasets (Kempny et al. 2026). In its authors’ evaluation, the criterion saved 17% of screening on average, and less where few records are relevant, so screening may cover most of the records. These evaluations draw on systematic reviews, so the criterion’s performance in a scoping review remains untested. Recall counts relevant records, so the criterion cannot show whether the unscreened records report factors that the screened records do not.

Data charting

Each included source is charted once into the form defined in the codebook, with one row for the source and one row for each factor it reports. The form is piloted on a sample of sources and revised before full charting begins.

The review does not appraise the included sources, following JBI guidance for scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2020). It maps the factors that sources report, and its eligible source types share no common appraisal tool.

Synthesis

The review codes factors inductively. The resulting taxonomy is a primary output. Counts by tier, field, source type, and year describe the evidence base, and the factor categories are compared across fields.

Artificial intelligence

No large language model makes or suggests eligibility, charting, or synthesis decisions. ASReview ranks records by predicted relevance, and the reviewers make every screening decision. The analysis reruns from the search exports without invoking any language model.

To estimate the screening workload, the first model in Table 1 also judged two sets of records the search returns, 300 drawn at random and the 489 that “research data management” in titles would add, against the screening guide’s criteria, with a prompt that states them. These judgments estimate workload only. They decide no record’s eligibility and enter neither screening nor charting.

A local large language model coded the sources included in the review by Chawinga and Zinn (2019) to identify those that meet this review’s eligibility criteria, which serve as known relevant sources for testing the query. For each source, it recorded the practice, data origin, field, source type, and reported factors from the title and abstract, with the passage that supports each code, choosing from the values listed in its prompt, which come from the eligibility criteria, the screening guide, and the codebook. Notebook 00 reports the codes and keeps the sources that meet the criteria, apart from publication year, as known sources. The model ran at temperature 0 with a fixed seed, which makes its output repeatable. A smaller model, run with the same prompt and settings, agrees with it on 79% to 100% of the codes for each variable, with Cohen’s kappa from 0.63 to 0.84.

Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, model identifier claude-opus-5-5, used through claude.ai from 11 to 18 November 2026) was used in search development. At the authors’ direction, it wrote the scripts, proposed measurements, and drafted the search development records; the authors chose each term in the query from the measurements. Reporting of AI use follows the joint position statement of Cochrane, the Campbell Collaboration, JBI, and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence (Flemyng et al. 2025).

Reporting and data

The checklists for PRISMA-ScR and PRISMA-S record where each item is reported. The flow diagram is generated from the screening counts. Search exports, screening decisions, charted data, and all code are in the repository.
