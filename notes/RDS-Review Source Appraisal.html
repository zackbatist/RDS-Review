1. Governance and reporting

These sources decide what a review that uses an LLM must disclose and where. They cover the AI section of the protocol, the statement of use in project documentation, and disclosure in the published paper’s Methods and cover letter. Only the joint statement and ICMJE carry authority, and ARISMA is the only source that addresses scoping reviews.

Source Access What it says Relevance to RDS-Review
Flemyng et al. 2025, joint position statement (Cochrane, Campbell, JBI, CEE). JBI Evid Synth 23(11):2162-2166 PMC Full text Based on RAISE (Table 1 cites “version 2.1 in development as of 22 September 2025”). Authors must declare AI that makes or suggests judgements, including eligibility, appraisal, extraction, synthesis across studies, certainty, and drafted text on strength of evidence or plain-language summaries. Reports give tool name, version and date; purpose and stages; validation and public inputs and outputs where practical; the tool’s funding and interests; limitations and biases. Prompts, outputs and code are shared “wherever possible”. AI decisions are “considered and reported as part of protocol development”, and the template paragraph opens “We will use…”. Grammar help generally needs no declaration. Nothing on scoping reviews or on AI-written code. The governing document for a JBI scoping review that uses AI. It sets what the protocol must say about AI decisions and what the published review must disclose. It does not address AI-drafted protocols or AI-written code directly.
RAISE 1 to 3, Thomas et al. (OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/FWAUD) OSF Blocked Read only through the joint statement, a Cochrane news item, a Cochrane-hosted explainer and Thomas’s July 2025 slides. No version number appears on any RAISE document. The framework the joint statement rests on. Not read directly, so its specific requirements cannot yet be cited.
Nussbaumer-Streit B, Streit S, Porritt K 2026. “Leveraging artificial intelligence for evidence synthesis.” JBI Evid Synth 24(7):1280-1282. doi:10.11124/JBIES-26-00371 DOI Full text (typeset PDF) Editorial with 14 references. AI shows “promise for procedural tasks such as citation screening, deduplication, and structured data extraction”. “Human oversight is essential.” Full automation is not yet possible and “should not be the aim”. Interpretive work needs “higher-order interpretive judgment”. Cites the joint statement twice. Does not mention trAIce or protocols. Porritt is a JBI employee and associate editor. The authors used Claude (Opus 4.7) for editing and say so. JBI’s own position. It supports AI for screening and extraction under human oversight and keeps interpretation human, which matches a design that limits the LLM to procedural tasks. Its authors disclosed using Claude for editing.
ICMJE Recommendations, January 2026, Section V and II.A.3, II.A.4, IV.A.3.d Web Full text AI cannot be an author; humans are responsible for submitted material. Authors disclose AI use at submission and describe it in the cover letter and the manuscript. Writing assistance goes in the acknowledgments. AI used for data collection, analysis or figures goes in the Methods with “the tool used, version, and prompts where applicable”. Non-disclosure “may be construed as misconduct”. Determines where AI-use disclosure must appear when the review is published, in the cover letter and the Methods, with tool, version and prompts.
Tri-Agency (CIHR, NSERC, SSHRC, CFI) generative AI guidance Web Full text, one reader; re-check blocked Covers developing and reviewing grant proposals only. The 2024 version required disclosure. The current FAQ states “there is no longer a blanket requirement for AI disclosure”. Applicants must still credit sources AI uncovered and stay solely accountable. Not relevant to the review itself. It covers grant proposals only, and its disclosure requirement has been dropped.
Tricco et al. 2018, PRISMA-ScR. Ann Intern Med 169(7):467-473. doi:10.7326/M18-0850 DOI Abstract only 20 essential and 2 optional items from a 24-member panel. Predates LLMs. The reporting checklist a scoping review follows. It has no items on AI use.
Tricco et al. 2026, “Updating the PRISMA reporting guideline for scoping reviews: a scoping review.” J Clin Epidemiol 196:112314. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112314 DOI Full text (appendices 1 to 13 unread) JBI-method scoping review, online 8 May 2026. 8,265 records screened, 43 documents, 37 candidate items for a Delphi survey. Searches were over 20 months old. The body mentions automation only as motivation and never mentions AI or LLMs. Shows PRISMA-ScR is being revised and that the revision has not yet taken up AI. Reviews report against the 2018 version until the update is published.
Holst et al. 2025, PRISMA-trAIce. JMIR AI 4:e80247. doi:10.2196/80247 DOI Full text (Multimedia Appendix 1 unread) Peer reviewed. Targeted review of EQUATOR AI guidelines with content analysis and thematic synthesis. Adapted from CONSORT-AI, TRIPOD-AI, GAMER, SPIRIT-AI, TRIPOD-LLM and DECIDE-AI. The text says 14 items; Table 1 has 17 rows (T1, A1, I1, M1 to M10, R1, R2, D1, D2). Not a Delphi, no user study. Extends PRISMA 2020; PRISMA-ScR is not mentioned. The flow diagram separates AI and human exclusions. The closest available checklist for documenting AI use in a review, so the likeliest template for an AI methods section. It is unendorsed and written for systematic reviews, not scoping reviews.
Moher D, Page M, McKenzie J, Takwoingi Y, Mayo-Wilson E 2026. “PRISMA-trAIce: A Name Without Endorsement.” JMIR AI 5:e104210 Page Full text Letter from five PRISMA Executive members, not peer reviewed, 18 August 2026. “We do not endorse it as a PRISMA checklist.” Item selection is poorly documented. PRISMA Executive members are developing consensus-based AI guidance that “will soon serve as an official resource, hosted on the PRISMA website”. Shows trAIce has no PRISMA Executive standing and that official PRISMA guidance on AI is coming. Reporting approaches built on trAIce may need to change when it appears.
Zabaleta M, Lin B 2026, PRISMA-LLM. arXiv:2609.11559 arXiv Full text Preprint, 10 September 2026. SciLitBench corpus of 888 review-automation papers with 14,726 annotations. Since 2023, 38.0% of software and product papers reported no evaluation against 9.3% of LLM papers. Of 118 positive-only LLM papers, 52% reported at least one unmet reliability bar. 16 items (LLM-1 to LLM-16) and five disclosure tiers (off-the-shelf software; prompt-only LLM; few-shot or structured workflow; retrieval, adapted or single-agent; multi-agent). The supplement lists 23 section-coded IDs with no mapping to the 16. No Delphi or user testing; not endorsed. Used alongside PRISMA 2020. PRISMA-ScR and JBI never appear. A second reporting framework for LLM use. Useful for spotting gaps in whichever AI methods section a review adopts. Also unendorsed.
Moghaddam MT, Alipour M 2026, ARISMA. arXiv:2608.25050 arXiv Full text (HTML) Preprint, 25 August 2026, University of Southern Denmark. Conceptual guideline with no empirical test. Six lifecycle phases, three AI-use categories (assistive, adjudicative, generative), a 22-item reporting checklist and an 11-row validation matrix. Developed through video consultations with 21 researchers and information specialists; no Delphi, no pilot. The protocol “must contain not only the ordinary review methods but also an AI methods appendix”. “A model-generated string that misses known studies on a pilot set is not a near miss; it is a failing search strategy.” Scoping reviews “may legitimately prioritize breadth, coding, and gap identification”. PRISMA-ScR is background only and JBI appears only as an organization and as SUMARI. “Seven components” appears in the abstract only. The only guideline that addresses scoping reviews and asks for an AI appendix in the protocol and a pilot-set check on LLM-built search strings. Both apply to any scoping review that uses an LLM in the protocol or search. It is an unvalidated proposal.
Fleurence RL et al. 2025, ELEVATE-GenAI. Value Health 28(11):1611-1625. doi:10.1016/j.jval.2025.06.018 DOI Partial. Published text blocked; arXiv:2501.12394 v2 read to character 100,000 of 134,544 HEOR-specific framework for evaluating generative AI output. Ten domains (model characteristics, accuracy, comprehensiveness, factuality, reproducibility and generalizability, robustness, fairness and bias, deployment context and efficiency, calibration and uncertainty, security and privacy). Targeted review of 522 records yielded 30 sources. Two case studies scored 24/30 and 25/30. No Delphi; agentic workflows not covered. A health-economics framework for judging generative AI output. Little direct bearing on a scoping review.
Lai H, Sousa-Pinto B, Cao C, Moher D, et al. 2026. J Clin Epidemiol 197:112383. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112383 DOI Full text (uploaded PDF; appendices 1 to 13 not included) Meta-epidemiological study registered on OSF (osf.io/8edhb). 21,880 records, 229 included studies and 440 tasks (title and abstract screening 103, extraction 94, search 48, risk of bias 40, full-text screening 33); 87.8% standalone validations; 80.3% peer reviewed; GPT in 81.1% of tasks. Adapted CHART (18 domains, 44 items) and adapted QUADAS-2 (four domains), each rated by two reviewers (kappa 0.934 and 0.807). Mean transparency 0.52 (SD 0.30). Weakest domains were protocol (0.12), implementation parameters (0.22), prompt engineering (0.30), ethics (0.32), query strategy (0.35) and performance definition (0.38). Full prompt templates scored 0.45 and sample size determination 0.02. Of 7,480 QUADAS-2 assessments, 60.6% were low risk, 6.4% high and 32.5% unclear. Test-set locking before optimization was unreported in 99.8% of tasks, separation of optimization from the test set was unclear in 77.7%, and 61.4% used a single evaluation set. Claims numbered 837 (66.8% weak); superiority in 64.8% of tasks and practice readiness in 47.5% (93.3% qualified); reproducibility or transparency caveats in 5.0%. The authors note CHART is a reporting guideline and not an appraisal tool, the QUADAS-2 adaptation is exploratory, and claims were coded from abstract, discussion and conclusion only. The abstract’s “model details (0.30)” does not match Figure 2, where model details score 0.60 and prompt engineering 0.30. The protocol domain is not defined in the main text (definitions are in Appendix 6) and sits among manuscript-level items such as ethics and funding, so it appears to concern the evaluation studies’ own protocols. Shows that published LLM performance figures are often unverifiable because test sets are not locked before prompts are tuned. That limits how far a review can rely on reported screening and extraction accuracy. It says nothing about LLM-written protocols.
Gray R et al. 2025. Nurs Rep 15(12):423. doi:10.3390/nursrep15120423 DOI Full text Editorial with an internal tally of one journal (649 documents, 116 reviews, 45 scoping reviews, 1,748 included documents, median 14 per review). Policy requires PRISMA-ScR and JBI, a justified method and stakeholder consultation, and that authors describe how any AI tools were implemented and validated. An editor’s expectations for scoping reviews, including a description of how any AI tools were implemented and validated. Indicates what a journal would check.

2. Scoping-review evidence

These are the sources closest to the design of a JBI scoping review. They bear on how eligibility criteria are written and tested and on any LLM help with screening. The evidence is small, rests on a handful of datasets, and touches JBI methods only in Lieberum and Khalil.

Source Access What it found Relevance to RDS-Review
Lieberum J-L et al. 2025. J Clin Epidemiol 181:111746. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111746 DOI Abstract of the published paper; full preprint (medRxiv 10.1101/2024.12.19.24319326), counts match JBI-guided scoping review with PRISMA-ScR and PCC, registered on OSF 4 March 2024. 8,054 hits, 37 included articles covering 10 of 13 review steps. Studies per step: search 15, selection 14, extraction 11, risk of bias 5, interpretation 4, question and criteria framing 3, qualitative synthesis 3, quantitative synthesis 2, publication 2, plain-language summary 1. None on deduplication, full-text retrieval or GRADE. GPT in 33 (89%); 21 (57%) validation studies. Authors rated LLM use promising in 20 (54%), neutral in 9, non-promising in 8. No critical appraisal; one extractor with a check. A JBI-guided scoping review on this same topic, so the nearest model for designing and registering a scoping review. Its step map shows where LLM evidence exists and where it does not.
Khalil H et al. 2024. Res Synth Methods 15(6):839-850. doi:10.1002/jrsm.1731 DOI Full text (repository PDF) JBI-authored guidance built from a scoping review of automation tools (60 studies, 70 tools, searched to June 2023). Stage-by-stage advice naming SUMARI, Covidence, Abstrackr, Rayyan, EPPI-Reviewer and ASReview. LLMs get one Discussion passage on ChatGPT (“Significant work is still needed”); none recommended. JBI’s guidance on automation in scoping reviews. It predates LLM tooling, so it supports human-led screening and extraction and gives no JBI position on LLMs.
Nordmann K, Fischer F 2026. BMC Health Serv Res 26(1):109. doi:10.1186/s12913-025-13901-4 DOI Full text (appendices unread) One completed scoping review (15,306 abstracts, 188 includes). “ChatGPT 4.0” through the API at temperature 0.7. Narrow, balanced and wide PCC criteria tested on a 25% subset (3,827). Wide criteria gave sensitivity 0.935 and specificity 0.627; narrow gave 0.692 and 0.863, PPV 0.189, workload saving 0.825. Three-prompt majority vote gave specificity 0.708 and balanced accuracy 0.795. Run-to-run kappa 0.76. Humans falsely included 3.56% and falsely excluded 0.36%. Scoping-review screening evidence. Shows that how eligibility criteria are written changes LLM screening accuracy a great deal, which matters for any LLM-drafted or LLM-tested criteria.
Nordmann K et al. 2026. JAMIA Open 9(1):ooaf098. doi:10.1093/jamiaopen/ooaf098 DOI Full text Same scoping review and dataset (15,307 records); not independent of the paper above. GPT-4.0 sensitivity 88% and 89%, specificity 67%, precision 11%, NPV 99%, workload saving 64%. Kappa between two GPT-4.0 runs 0.76, against humans 0.12 to 0.13, human pairs 0.29. Cost $505.72 (GPT-4.0) and $9.06 (GPT-3.5). Rayyan decided 4 abstracts. Feasibility numbers and a cost figure for ChatGPT screening of a scoping review. It reuses the dataset of the paper above.
Ito Y et al. 2025. Cureus 17(8):e90026. doi:10.7759/cureus.90026 DOI Full text (appendices unread) Simulation on one completed scoping review (delirium in cancer). GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4o and o1 gave sensitivity/specificity of 0.43/0.99, 0.71/0.97 and 1.00/0.91 against the title and abstract reference, with PPV 0.23, 0.11 and 0.05 and NPV 1.00. Against the full-text reference, sensitivity was 1.00 for all. No consistency testing. JBI not mentioned. Shows that model choice alone can move sensitivity from 0.43 to 1.00 in a scoping review, so any LLM screening step needs a pilot on the chosen model.
Krag CH et al. 2024. medRxiv 10.1101/2024.10.01.24314702 DOI Full text STARD design on a neuroradiology AI systematic review (492 analyzed, 12 includes) and a diabetic kidney imaging scoping review (494 analyzed). Six models, zero-shot. Systematic review sensitivity was 33 to 50% for GPT-4T, GPT-4o and Opus at 97 to 99% specificity; Haiku reached 100% and 58%. Scoping review sensitivity was 74 to 84%. Run-to-run kappa 0.51 to 0.96 (review 1) and 1.0 for GPT-4o (review 2). No precision. The Discussion cites 49.2 h and the Results 5.2 h. Shows sensitivity can be low when includes are few and that run-to-run agreement varies by model. Both matter when the inclusion rate is low.
Soprano M et al. 2025, AIDME. ICTIR ’25:194-207. doi:10.1145/3731120.3744586 DOI Full text Pipeline demonstration on fact-checking literature with GPT-4o-mini: 12,259 records, 497 retained (about 4%), 357 after topic filtering. Time saved estimated at about 110 h and 225 h. No recall, precision, agreement or repeat runs. Maps to PRISMA 2020, not PRISMA-ScR. The reported counts do not reconcile (12,259 minus 497 is 11,762, not 11,935). An example of an AI-aided scoping pipeline. It reports no accuracy figures, so it is no evidence of reliability.
Borges FT et al. 2026. Int J Environ Res Public Health 23(1):81. doi:10.3390/ijerph23010081 DOI Full text Identified by author, year and topic. GPT-4o-mini screened titles only, zero-shot in Portuguese, in batches of 10, with “Maybe” counted as include. 1,120 screened, 349 included after human review of every model-included record. No recall, precision or workload figures. Retrospective registration. PRISMA-ScR only. A scoping review that used an LLM for title screening with human review of every include. Shows the practice exists. Reports no accuracy.
Holm S et al. 2026. Eur Burn J 7(1):4. doi:10.3390/ebj7010004 DOI Full text Identified by author, year and topic. Screening and extraction were human and in duplicate. ChatGPT 5.0 only organized extracted data into a decision tree, unvalidated. A scoping review where the LLM only organized extracted data. No bearing on screening or extraction.

3. Screening, extraction and appraisal evidence

These sources set expectations for any LLM step in a review, such as screening, charting or extraction. They shape how a pilot calibration or a codebook pilot is designed and what accuracy to expect.

Source Access What it found Relevance to RDS-Review
Tripathi et al. 2026. Am J Surg DOI Full text (uploaded PDF) Gemini 2.0 Flash screened 3,214 PubMed abstracts and found 10 of 10 known RCTs. Specificity 99.3%. About 1 h against 96 to 110 h manually. One database, abstracts only, 10-trial reference set, 22 of 34 LLM includes off target. The source of a pilot-and-calibrate approach to LLM screening, with a hand-screened sample and review of excludes as well as includes.
Bayani et al. 2025. Int J Med Inform DOI Full text (uploaded PDF) Mistral 7B, Vicuna 13B and Llama 3.2 on a disability and AI scoping review. Best screening accuracy 66%, full text 65%, extraction up to about 90% agreement. The LLM proposed extra charting dimensions. Small open models; results sections removed before extraction. The closest precedent for an LLM suggesting charting dimensions, which bears on developing a charting codebook.
Xie et al. 2026. J Evid Based Med DOI Full text (uploaded PDF) 18 screening studies, OSF registered. Title and abstract sensitivity 0.92, specificity 0.94, I² 96 to 100%. Four full-text studies pooled to 0.99. The best summary of how well LLMs screen titles and abstracts, with very high heterogeneity. Sets expectations for any LLM screening.
Laignelot F et al. 2026. J Clin Epidemiol 194:112221. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112221 DOI Abstract only Systematic review of 63 studies and 148 LLM assessments (GPT 114, 77%). Title and abstract screening 78 (53%), extraction 23 (16%), full-text screening 20 (14%). Title and abstract median PPA 0.92 (IQR 0.69 to 0.98) and NPA 0.89 (0.72 to 0.95). Full text PPA 0.93 and NPA 0.92. Extraction (n=11) median accuracy 0.95. Risk of bias (n=6) median accuracy 0.62. Post-GPT-4 models seemed better. Summarizes performance by review step. Extraction looks strong and risk-of-bias appraisal weak, which matters little for a scoping review, where appraisal is optional.
Cao C et al. 2025. Ann Intern Med 178(3):389-401. doi:10.7326/ANNALS-24-02189 DOI Abstract only; partial medRxiv precursor (10.1101/2024.06.01.24308323) Separate paper from otto-SR. Ten reviews; 48,425 citations at abstract stage and 12,690 free full texts; GPT4-0125-preview. Abstract screening weighted sensitivity 97.7% and specificity 85.2%; full text 96.5% and 91.2%. Zero-shot sensitivity about 49%. Cost per 10,000 citations $157.02 against $1,666.67 for one human screener. The preprint adds text-only input and untested scoping reviews as limits. Shows that prompt design strongly affects LLM screening accuracy, with zero-shot sensitivity near 49%. Relevant to how a screening prompt is developed.
Delgado-Chaves FM et al. 2025. PNAS 122(2):e2411962122. doi:10.1073/pnas.2411962122 DOI Full text (appendix unread) 18 LLMs (3 OpenAI, 15 open-source via Ollama) on three reviews of 4,501, 1,650 and 66 records. Mean agreement with the first human reviewer 92%, 88% and 40%. Best mean MCC 0.349 (GPT-4o). The authors call recall and F1 “rather poor”. No sensitivity reported in the text. Shows open-source models can screen but that missed records go unmeasured. Relevant if screening uses local models.
Wang Z et al. 2025, LEADS. Nat Commun 16:8361. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-62058-5 DOI Full text Mistral-7B fine-tuned on 633,759 instructions from 21,335 reviews. Search recall 24.68 against about 20.9 for the best baseline. Screening Recall@50 of 85 against 86 for GPT-4o. Extraction accuracy 0.68 against 0.55 (study characteristics) and 0.78 against 0.45 (trial results). Screening user study: recall 0.81 against 0.78, time 449 s against 580 s. Participant counts are inconsistent. Trial-oriented. A locally deployable fine-tuned model for screening and extraction, built for clinical trials. Limited fit outside clinical-trial topics.
Merchán-Cruz et al. 2026. Informatics DOI Full text (uploaded PDF) Agentic review of 122 studies. Screening and extraction tractable under supervision; appraisal near chance. Temperature disclosed in under 2% and prompts in under 10%. Table 12 reports PRISMA-adherence overestimates of 23.1 to 29.7% citing Forero. Protocol written after the search; inconsistent counts. A landscape review of agentic review tools. Its finding that prompts and settings are rarely disclosed shows what an AI methods section should contain. Its counts need checking.
Forero DA et al. 2025. J Med Syst 49(1):80. doi:10.1007/s10916-025-02212-0 DOI Full text (supplement unread) GPT-4o, DeepSeek V3, Gemini 2.0 Flash and Qwen 2.5 Max scored 20 acupuncture reviews against PRISMA 2020 and 20 overviews against PRIOR. Mean PRISMA adherence was 61.2% for experts and 84.3 to 90.6% for the LLMs. Mean differences were 29.7, 29.4, 23.2 and 28.1 percentage points (all p < 0.001). Item-level accuracy 60.5 to 70.4%. Shows LLMs overstate how well reviews follow PRISMA by 23 to 30 points, so a reporting-checklist self-check should stay human.
Fleurence et al. 2026. Value Health (in press) DOI Full text (uploaded PDF) ISPOR task force rapid review of 115 studies. Search drafting needs librarian validation; end-to-end automation unreliable. PubMed only, single screener. ISPOR’s rapid review. Supports the view that LLM-written search strategies need librarian validation.

4. Protocol-stage, search and reference evidence

This section covers what LLMs do when they draft review components. It tests protocol-stage tasks, research questions and frameworks, search strings and references, and it indicates where an LLM draft is most exposed.

Source Access What it found Relevance to RDS-Review
Stewart-Evans J et al. 2025. arXiv:2507.06623 arXiv Full text Preprint. Claude 3.5 Sonnet on 10 sources and 8 data items, with the scoping protocol as the prompt. Simple against extended approach (micro averages): precision 90.6% and 95.9%, recall 16.4% and 24.1%, F1 27.8% and 38.5%. Macro recall 38.3% and 46.3%. Key-findings accuracy 9.6% and 15.8%. It caught 2 of 39 deliberate errors. Single reviewer reference, no train/test split. Tests a scoping protocol as the prompt for extraction. The LLM handled citation fields, missed most open-ended content and caught almost none of the planted errors, so it cannot be trusted to check a protocol.
Morrone M et al. 2026. Eur J Appl Physiol 126(4):1907-1916. doi:10.1007/s00421-025-06100-w DOI Full text Gemini 2.5 Pro with Deep Research produced one scoping review (cross-education) from a title and objective on 11 April 2025. Four experts reviewed the first complete output unedited, qualitatively and by consensus. It failed to hierarchize evidence, state specific research questions or follow Arksey and O’Malley, Levac and Peters. PRISMA-ScR adherence was formal only. Of 62 citations, 52 were open access. Plagiarism was flagged. The authors double as the experts. The closest failure analysis for an LLM-written scoping review. Experts found the research question, framework and evidence hierarchy wrong, which are core protocol-stage tasks.
Wang S, Scells H, Koopman B, Zuccon G 2023. arXiv:2302.03495 arXiv Full text (references unread) ChatGPT (January 2023) against CLEF TAR (72 topics) and a 40-topic seed collection. Original authored queries: precision 0.0207, recall 0.8317. ChatGPT prompt q2: precision 0.1166, recall 0.1310. Objective baseline recall 0.9128. Seed-guided prompt recall 0.5171 against 0.1290. Ten reruns varied about 12% in recall. Venue unverified. Shows ChatGPT-written search strings miss many studies. Relevant to any search strategy an LLM drafts.
Wang S et al. 2025. arXiv:2505.07155 arXiv Full text Reproduction with ten LLMs. o1 recall 0.4341 to 0.6545 across prompts against 0.8436 for manual queries. Combining queries from all seed studies raised recall to 0.6978 to 0.7361 and cut precision to 0.0002 to 0.0087. Seed selection “plays the biggest role”. Contamination from public test collections is possible. Venue unverified. Confirms the recall gap with newer models. Seed studies help only when well chosen.
Chelli M et al. 2024. J Med Internet Res 26:e53164. doi:10.2196/53164 DOI Full text 11 rotator cuff reviews, 471 references. Hallucination 39.6% (GPT-3.5), 28.6% (GPT-4) and 91.4% (Bard). Recall 11.9%, 13.7% and 0%. 2023 models without retrieval. Measures fabricated references from older models. Relevant to any bibliography or known-item list assembled with LLM help.
Walters WH, Wilder EI 2023. Sci Rep 13:14045. doi:10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5 DOI Full text 42 topics, 636 citations. 55% of 222 GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated; 70% of GPT-4 book-chapter citations. Real citations carried substantive errors in 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4). The same reference-fabrication problem in earlier models. Supports checking every DOI.
Linardon J et al. 2025. JMIR Ment Health 12:e80371. doi:10.2196/80371 DOI Full text (appendices unread) GPT-4o in June 2025, 176 citations, 35 (19.9%) fabricated. Depression 4/68, binge eating 17/60, body dysmorphic disorder 14/48. Of 141 real citations, 77 (54.6%) were error-free. Fabrication rises on niche topics. Shows GPT-4o still fabricated about one citation in five, and more on niche topics, so a specialized topic carries more risk.

5. End-to-end pipelines

These systems automate whole reviews with LLM agents. They show what end-to-end claims look like and how little validation supports them for scoping reviews, and they set the contrast with workflows that keep the LLM off the data path.

Source Access What it found Relevance to RDS-Review
Cao C et al., otto-SR. medRxiv 10.1101/2025.06.13.25329541 (v1 June 2025; v4 4 May 2026) DOI Full text (v4 HTML cut off mid-Phase 2) Preprint; no journal version found. GPT-4.1 and o3-mini-high. Screening on 5 reviews (32,357 citations), extraction on 7 reviews (4,495 data points), 12 of the 14 Cochrane reviews in the April 2024 issue. Body figures: abstract-stage sensitivity 96.6% against 87.3% for humans; full-text 96.2% against 63.3%. The abstract’s 96.7% against 81.7% uses human full-text sensitivity after dropping one review (44.1%). The 97.9% specificity does not appear in the Results. Extraction 93.1% against 79.7%. “Two days” appears only in the v1 abstract and an ISPOR abstract. LLM-as-judge with blinded human adjudication. No cost data. Authors hold equity in the company (otto review, LLC in v1; Otto Science Institute in v4) and one works at OpenAI. The strongest claim for end-to-end review automation, from a preprint with commercial conflicts. Shows what such a pipeline claims, not that it works for scoping reviews.
Lin H-T, Yeh J-T 2026, meta-pipe. arXiv:2606.28363 arXiv Full text Preprint. A 10-stage pipeline on Opus 4 and Haiku 3.5 with R (meta, metafor, gemtc, netmeta) and Quarto. Five mandatory human points (PICO, screening disagreements, analysis type, GRADE, interpretation). Self-described as “a system description, not a validation study”. The $15 to $30 cost is a projection from token pricing. The arXiv identifier and submission date (5 April 2026) do not agree. 6. Reproducibility, code and autonomous research

These sources bear on LLM-written analysis code and on whether results can be rerun. They show how far such code can be trusted and why keeping model calls out of the analysis path matters.

Dunn CW, Schultz DT, Musser JM 2026. Patterns 7(9):101644. doi:10.1016/j.patter.2026.101644 DOI Full text Tutorial. An LLM is on the data path if the analysis needs it at run time and off it if it only produced durable artifacts such as code. 8. Relevance in brief

Each item names an aspect of an LLM-assisted scoping review and what the sources say about it.

  • Disclosing LLM use. Only the joint statement and ICMJE carry authority. Both want tool, version, prompts and purpose reported in the protocol and the Methods. The reporting checklists are unendorsed proposals, and official PRISMA guidance is in development.
  • Drafting the protocol. No study benchmarks an LLM-written scoping protocol, and the nearest evidence is unfavourable. An unedited LLM scoping review failed on question, framework and evidence hierarchy (Morrone), and a protocol used as a prompt caught 2 of 39 planted errors (Stewart-Evans).
  • Search strategy and references. LLM-written search strings reach recall of 0.13 to 0.65 against 0.83 to 0.91 for manual queries (Wang). Between 18% and 55% of the references GPT models supplied were fabricated or hallucinated across three studies (Walters and Wilder, Chelli, Linardon).
  • Eligibility criteria and screening. Pooled and median sensitivity sits near 0.92 (Xie, Laignelot), yet one scoping review ranged from 0.43 to 1.00 across models (Ito). How the criteria are worded matters as much as the model (Nordmann and Fischer, McLaughlin). Run-to-run agreement is rarely reported (Krag, Nordmann).
  • Charting and extraction. Structured fields reach a median accuracy of 0.95 (Laignelot), while open-ended content is far weaker, at 10 to 16% for key findings (Stewart-Evans). Risk-of-bias appraisal is weak, with a median of 0.62, but optional in scoping reviews. Bayani is the one scoping-review precedent for LLM-suggested charting dimensions.
  • Analysis code and reproducibility. Repeating an LLM analysis gives different results (Cui and Alexander), and LLM-written code needs independent checking (METR, Beel). The recommended design keeps the LLM off the data path so the analysis reruns with no model (Dunn).
  • Trusting the published numbers. Evaluations report prompts and parameters thinly, and 99.8% of tasks do not report locking the test set before prompt tuning (Lai).
  • What is missing. No benchmark of an LLM-drafted scoping protocol exists, and no official reporting standard for AI use in scoping reviews. The PRISMA-ScR update is unpublished and its supporting review does not mention AI. Scoping-review screening evidence comes from five papers on four datasets, and the one JBI-authored automation paper predates LLMs (Khalil).
